Node City · Build v120w9

The city got real

Windows reflect the sky and have rooms behind them. Things cast shadows on the ground they stand on. Land is worth different amounts and that decides what opens on it. Businesses now compete for your lots — and some of them fail.

How we built this

Worth a paragraph, because it explains why the notes below are full of numbers.

Every change in this update was graded against real Cities: Skylines II screenshots across twelve categories — by a reviewer who did not do the work, scoring the actual game rather than a mock-up. Nineteen passes. The average went from 3.6 to 6.9 out of 10.

The rule we held ourselves to: never ship a number with no model behind it, and never ship a rule nobody enforces. Where you see a figure in the game — a demand percentage, a land value, a citizen's age — something computes it. Where we couldn't compute it honestly, the panel says so instead of inventing one.

Twice this update, that discipline killed our own work. A land-value fix would have silently emptied three district types of everything they build; a “regression” in the ground textures turned out to be us comparing two numbers that were never the same quantity. Both were caught by measuring instead of trusting.

The city looks different

Ten changes, in rough order of what you'll notice first.

Glass is glass now

The city had no reflections at all. Every window was a flat dark rectangle — a painted panel, not a pane. Now there's a real environment map, built from the game's own sky, so the reflection follows the time of day, the weather and the haze without anyone maintaining a second copy of it.

Then we put rooms behind the glass: a sky band across the head of each pane falling into interior darkness, with a floor line at the sill. A window reads as a window into somewhere.

+0 triangles+0 draw calls The interior costs a 2 KB texture. The reflection rebuilds only when the sky actually changes — 292 times a day, none of them between 9pm and 4am.

Things sit on the ground

Lamps, bins, benches, bollards, parked cars and people cast no shadow at all — so they read as stickers laid on the pavement. They have contact shadows now, and they darken the road's existing grain rather than covering it with a grey blob, so they look like shade instead of a decal.

It works at night without a switch: the shadow is a multiplier, so a fraction of a dim pavement is simply a dim pavement.

+2 draw calls, whole city Two meshes cover every prop, vehicle and pedestrian in the map.

You can see the far side of the map

Distance haze was set to 30 units. The far corner of the district is 30.18 units from the camera. It wasn't fading distance — it was dissolving the city, and grass and asphalt out there had become the same grey. Fixed by measurement, and the near field is untouched.

Road markings lie flat

Every zebra bar and stop line was a little slab standing proud of the road, with a lit wall on one side and a dark one on the other. A paint slab was a box, and a box has walls. It's a flat quad now — and every marking in the city went from 24 vertices to 4.

The kerb was purple

On every street, every tree pit and every plot edge — a lilac line running beside a warm concrete pavement. Nobody had spotted it. Repainted to the pavement's own tone, with brightness unchanged, so nothing tuned against it needed re-tuning.

The ground is ground

Open land between roads was one unbroken flat green. It now carries grass tussocks, scrub, rock and saplings in clumps — with bald ground between them, which is what makes the clumps read as thickets rather than a sprinkle. Plus dirt staining and worn margins where grass meets paving.

The tufts are splayed blades, not blobs. We tried spheres first and they photographed as a field of green stones.

People are people-sized

Nine rounds of grading missed this: every citizen in the city was about twice human size. We didn't fix it by eye. The city already contains an object whose scale nobody disputes — a car — and a citizen stood 2.16× the height of a car's roof, where a real person is 1.17×. Holding that one ratio true is the whole correction.

A new Duel Arena

Rebuilt from the ground up: a glazed concourse, a set-back drum with an occupied roof terrace, a cantilevered canopy, a floating halo and lit signage carrying a crossed-cards mark. At night the whole elevation lights up and it's the brightest thing on the street.

It was also, embarrassingly, in no screenshot we had ever taken — it sat 33 pixels behind a UI panel and off the edge of the frame. It has its own camera angle now.

Office blocks exist

The demand panel had been showing office demand for months and the game had no office building — zoning office got you a research spire. There's a proper one now: freestanding and set back, taller than its plot is wide, with a glass ribbon, mullion fins, a coloured accent panel and a surface car park out front. From the air you can now tell offices from shops from industry without reading a single label.

Buildings meet their ground

Every building stood on its plot at a hard corner with nothing in the join. There's a kerbed foundation bed with planting against the wall now. While measuring this we found the plot layer had been laying a second pavement on top of five buildings' own — and a green lawn quadrant across the Med Lab's plaza. Removing that saved a draw call for the entire city.

The city plays differently

The zoning economy, and what it means for how you build.

Land is worth different amounts, and it decides what opens

Every plot now has a value built from things the city already models: what's nearby, who lives around it, whether a bus actually stops there, whether it's on the water, and how dirty the air is. That sorts the map into five bands.

The bands aren't a readout — they decide what develops. Paint the same Commercial zone on cheap land and expensive land and you get different businesses: a food truck on one, a restaurant on the other. Paint it on land too cheap for anything that zone builds and the plot stays empty, and the game tells you why in plain words.

There is no rule anywhere saying “luxury retail needs land worth more than X.” Luxury refuses a cheap lot because nothing a luxury street is made of will open on land that cheap. The floor shown in the panel is worked out from the live map every time it's drawn.

Districts, not just zones

Once researched, a block stops being generically “Commercial.” You tell it what kind: shops, restaurants, nightlife, luxury. Industry splits into general works, heavy industry and logistics. Offices into technology, financial and corporate.

Three of them are card districts — Mythic Retail, Mythic Entertainment and Card Manufacturing. The last one isn't a themed skin: a paper mill, a printworks and a packing depot are the literal ingredients of a booster pack in this game's own supply chain.

NPC companies compete for your lots

You don't place every business any more. You zone, and companies bid. Each one weighs customers nearby, ground rent, how many rivals are already trading within a few tiles, and whether transit serves the plot. The best bid takes the lot — and if nobody clears the reserve, the plot stays empty and the game names what sank it.

Rent can put a business under

This is new and it's the part we're happiest with. Firms now pay ground rent priced off the land their plot actually sits on. A shop that was comfortably profitable on cheap ground can be squeezed out when the neighbourhood around it becomes valuable — and the ledger says so, in as many words:

Card Shop went bankrupt. Ground rent took 1,893 🔥 — it was 1,012 🔥 in profit before the rent.

That line only prints when it's actually true. A business that was losing money on its trading anyway doesn't get to blame the landlord.

Businesses stopped dying every sixty days

They were, and the reason turned out to be a missing arrow rather than a missing pile of money. The city held 190× what a shopfront needed and had no way to reach it: money that landed in an established firm's till could be spent on wages, stock and upkeep, and could never be invested. New businesses now raise their start-up capital from established firms' surplus.

A side effect worth calling out: the distress states — Reduced, Layoffs, Debt, Default — had literally never once been seen, because every business died instantly. You can watch a firm struggle now.

A research tree

Zone types, district specialisations, licences and buildings unlock through research instead of all being available in minute one. Licences you buy at City Hall are wired into it, so holding the licence and holding the land it applies to are one progression. When something's locked, the message names the node that opens it and what it costs.

Citizens have ages and careers

Click a citizen and see who they are, where they live, where they work, how they're doing — and now how old they are and how far up they've got at work. Both advance with the city clock.

The age is marked with a ≈ on the panel, and that's deliberate: it's a sample drawn from the city's real age distribution, not a fact about that person, and the panel says which. There's a fourth label on that screen now — LIVE, DERIVED, SAMPLED, or plainly UNAVAILABLE — so you always know what kind of thing you're reading.

Zoning tells you why nothing is happening

Zoning a district in a city that isn't feeding itself used to change nothing visible, with no explanation. The rule hasn't been softened. What changed is that the game says so, and the threshold in the sentence is read from the same model that enforces it:

Zoned, but nothing will move in — Health is 0% and growth needs 90%. Build a Clinic.

Also in this build

  • Power import and export over your highway connection, with batteries drawn down first — a round trip to the neighbours and back loses you the spread twice.
  • The city budget, every line named and reconciling to the treasury. Two lines a city builder would normally show are deliberately missing, with a note, because our ledger genuinely can't separate them yet.
  • Buses and trains — a Bus Company and a Rail Operator bought from City Hall, then built out yourself with stops, stations and routes.
  • Emergency Broadcast, the in-game phone feed.
  • Named streets and traffic — click a road, name it, see 24 hours of flow.
  • Electricity, water and pollution as real systems: nine plant types, per-city hydrology, and plumes that drift downwind into your groundwater.

Still rough, honestly

You'd find these anyway. Better you hear them from us.

Shown side by side with a real CS2 screenshot, you'd still pick the real one instantly

What gives us away has changed, though, and it's no longer the rendering. It's the composition: about 40% of an aerial shot is empty green field, and the built area is a hard-edged rectangle with nothing beyond it. A real city runs off all four edges of the frame. No amount of material work touches that.

Every window carries the same reflection

Convincing on one pane, a giveaway on fifty. Two office blocks three metres apart have indistinguishable glass, because the interior is a painted profile rather than an actual reflection of what's standing in front of it.

Trees are still spheres on sticks

The conifers are genuinely good — layered tiers, real silhouette, autumn colour. The deciduous trees have no branch structure and no trunk taper, and the field bushes read as rocks.

Roads are dead straight

Every junction square, every plot one unit wide, every lamp at the same offset. The grid reads as a grid. Curved roads are a much bigger change than they sound.

Carriageways are narrow

About 4 metres against a real residential street's 5 to 5.5. It only looks right because the cars are drawn narrow too. Widening it re-cuts every junction in the game, so it's its own piece of work.

A lamp post's shadow is a smudge at its foot

Deliberate, and we measured the alternative: resolving a mast that thin properly needs a second shadow cascade, which costs more than it's worth. Cars and people get real directional shadows; thin poles get grounding instead.

Everything here runs in the actual game — where we say something works, it's because we booted it and looked at it, and where we say it doesn't, that's why it's in the last section instead of the first.
